excerpt: >
  Hospital cost shifting—recouping public payment cuts by charging private payers more—is a claim basic economics rejects. We show why, and when the rejection fails. With the set of hospitals fixed, a public-payment cut moves neither the surplus a hospital and insurer bargain over nor the power dividing it, so commercial prices do not move, whatever the insurer's market power. Cost-shifting requires that the cut threaten a hospital's survival: the insurer then either keeps it open at a higher price, or lets it close while its surviving rivals raise theirs. Pass-through appears only near exit and is zero for a secure hospital's own price. Empirically, the commercial-price response does not scale with insurer market power, but rises with a hospital's predicted closure risk, and closures raise rivals' prices. The exit margin can reconcile a mixed literature.
